Galaxy on AnVIL with real jobs: Batch, a single node, and a warm pool

Three ways of running Galaxy jobs were measured on the same 2 GB sequencing inputs: GCP Batch with one VM per job, everything on a single 32-vCPU node, and Batch with a warm pool of reusable VMs. The work was 108 real tools submitted as one user and as four users at once, plus three IWC workflows. Every number on this page is from a run that happened; nothing is projected.

Executive summary

  • For one user, the single node is fastest and cheapest. The 108-tool matrix took 77 minutes and $2.24 on the no-Batch node, 99 minutes and $3.41 on Batch, and 94 minutes and $5.37 on the warm pool. The node reads its data from local disk; Batch VMs read it from the head node over NFS, which is where most of the difference comes from.
  • For four users at once, only Batch holds its pace. Batch ran four copies of the matrix in 110 minutes, 10% longer than one copy, for 3.4 times the money. The single node had 90% of the work done after 179 minutes when its Galaxy stopped answering; its queue-wait tail reached 45 minutes against 7 on Batch. Per user the node was still cheaper, $1.30 against $2.90.
  • Batch pays an NFS tax that grows with load. CPU-bound aligners run as fast on a Batch VM as on the node. Tools that stream their 2 GB input run 1.7 to 4 times slower on Batch, and 1.5 to 2.3 times slower again when four users share one head node. fastqc: 110 s on the node, 414 s on a Batch VM, 608 s on a Batch VM with four users.
  • The warm pool costs 40 to 57% more than plain Batch for the same wall-clock. At TTL 600 s with 4 idle VMs it reused a third of the jobs and cut the median queue wait by 15 to 20 s, while idle VMs added 5 to 10 VM-hours per level.
  • Two confounds were found and corrected during the day. The no-Batch configuration had been giving every tool a quarter of its cores, which is fixed and excluded from the matrix figures. A default 19 GB disk quota paused the workflow runs for up to 40 minutes, so the workflow wall-clocks below are upper bounds.

What was run

The inputs are the 2 GB tier of the benchmarking-inputs bucket: paired whole-genome reads with a GenBank reference, paired RNA-seq reads with the hg38 gene annotation, and paired ChIP-seq reads. Two kinds of work ran on them.

The tool matrix is 114 single-tool jobs covering 108 of the 260 tools in AnVIL's tool set, arranged as a dependency graph: five aligners on the reads; samtools, picard, bamtools and deeptools on the alignments; freebayes, lofreq and bcftools on the variants; MACS2 and bedtools on the peaks; featureCounts and StringTie on the RNA-seq alignment; and the text and collection utilities on everything's reports. Submitting the graph once is one user's worth of work. Submitting it four times at once, each in its own history, is four users. Each level ran on a freshly deployed instance of each configuration.

The workflows are three IWC workflows run as a user would run them: variant calling on the whole-genome reads (12 tools), the RNA-seq paired-end workflow (20 steps) and ChIP-seq paired-end peak calling (7 tools), launched together on each configuration.

Where the time goes

Queue wait

Queue wait is the time between Galaxy creating a job and the job starting to execute. For one user it is dominated by the Batch VM boot: a median of 29 s on Batch, where 70 of the 121 jobs ran as pods on the head node and the rest waited about 80 s for a VM, against 27 s on the node, where every job is a pod and the wait is Kubernetes scheduling. The warm pool's 14 s is the effect of reusing a VM for a third of the jobs. For four users the picture inverts: Batch's median rises to 112 s and its 95th percentile to 7 minutes as VMs are provisioned in bulk, while the node's median stays at 90 s but its 95th percentile reaches 45 minutes, which is the aligners queuing behind the 24-core cap.

The pool creates fewer VMs and keeps each one longer. A VM held for its 600 s TTL after every job, four of them per machine shape, is paid for whether or not another job arrives, and in this workload one did only a third of the time. Wall-clock was within 5 minutes of plain Batch at both levels, so the extra VM-hours bought nothing a user would notice.

The workflows

The three IWC workflows ran once on each configuration, launched together, before the matrix. Two caveats apply to their timings and keep them out of the headline figures. A default 19.3 GB disk quota on every image paused jobs for up to 40 minutes mid-run before it was found and raised, so each wall-clock is an upper bound. And the no-Batch run predates the core fix described below: its tools ran with a quarter of their threads, which is why bowtie2 took 125 minutes there against 32 on Batch.

Two things the runs exposed

The single node had been running with a quarter of its cores. The deployment's values give Kubernetes jobs a CPU request of a quarter core per TPV core so that small jobs fit on the head node's spare CPU, and Galaxy's Kubernetes runner sets a tool's thread count from that request. On the no-Batch branch every tool runs as a Kubernetes job, so bwa_mem had been running with 2 threads instead of 8, STAR and freebayes with 3 instead of 10. The no-batch mixin now requests the full core count. The matrix figures on this page are from runs with that fix; the workflow figures are not.

Galaxy on the single node stopped answering under four users. After 179 minutes of the four-user level, with about 30 jobs queued behind the core cap, the head node's Galaxy web process lost its message-queue connection and refused API connections for several minutes. It recovered on its own, but the driver collecting results gave up in the meantime, which is why that level is reported from a snapshot taken shortly before. The Batch head nodes, a smaller VM carrying the same Galaxy, never faltered, because their job execution and its memory and I/O happen elsewhere.

What broke on the way

Getting these numbers took four rounds of runs. Each round found a defect in the harness or the deployment, all since fixed in galaxy-tests or on the galaxy-k8s-boot branches.

  • A 19.3 GB default disk quota paused every job once the 2 GB inputs and the first alignments exceeded it. The benchmark now raises it to 400 GB before staging.
  • The run cleanup step deleted every active Batch job in the project created after its own run began, so three finishing runs wiped out the first level of two runs deployed 30 minutes later. It now deletes only the Batch jobs its own Galaxy lists as active.
  • Cancelling a run left its head node and about 30 Batch jobs running. Runs are now left to fail rather than cancelled, and the steps that could start work after a cancel no longer do.
  • Staged collections were not named what the workload files assumed, and a paired-collection input needs the list mapped over it with the paired type named; both are handled in the matrix definitions and driver.
  • A GitHub incident returned 504 on release downloads for an hour, failing three deployments while they fetched the NFS provisioner chart.
  • Tool-level: bwa_mem2 has no human index in the CVMFS data; two of 435 warm-pool jobs lost their stdout on a reused VM; one of four samtools fastx pods died at its 4 GB memory limit sorting a 2 GB BAM.

Recommendations

  1. For one user, or for cost, run on a single node. It was the fastest and cheapest configuration for one user's work, 77 minutes and $2.24 against Batch's 99 and $3.41, and the cheapest per user at four. Its limits are the 24-core cap, which serialises concurrent users, and Galaxy sharing the node with the jobs, which failed under four users.
  2. For several concurrent users, use Batch without a pool. It held four users to a 10% wall-clock increase at $2.90 per user. Its remaining inefficiency is the NFS tax: I/O-bound tools run 1.7 to 4 times slower on a Batch VM than on local disk, and slower still as users are added. Moving job inputs off the head node, to an object store or a faster shared filesystem, is the largest improvement available to this configuration.
  3. Turn the warm pool off. At the settings most favourable to it, 600 s and 4 idle VMs, it reused a third of jobs and saved 15 to 20 s of median wait while adding 40 to 57% to the Batch bill, with the same wall-clock.
  4. Check core allocation before comparing destinations. The quarter-core CPU request silently gave the no-Batch configuration a quarter of its threads for two days of experiments. The galaxy_slots job metric shows what a job really got.
  5. Fix the three failing tools before the next matrix run. MultiQC 1.35's stderr rule, bwa_mem2's missing index and the deeptools plotEnrichment command-build failure each cost the same entries on every configuration.
